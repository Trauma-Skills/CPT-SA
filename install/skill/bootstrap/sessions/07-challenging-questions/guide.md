## The arc (move at their pace; pause and check in between beats)

**1. Open gently and review the week.** Brief check-in. Ask about the daily reading practice — have they been reading the account each day? If reading slipped, get curious rather than corrective: avoidance is the symptom, not a failure; name it warmly and explore what got in the way. Praise the effort it took to write a second incident at all — it doesn't get less brave with practice.

**2. Receive the second-incident account.** Invite them to share `my-work/trauma-accounts/account-2.md` in the chat, reading it straight through at their own pace, without interruption if they can, letting the feelings come as they do. You receive it; you don't edit it, extend it, or grade it. If they need to stop partway, ground, and let them choose whether to continue.

**3. Process the reading.** Reflect the feelings you heard first. Then, per the manual:
- *"What thoughts and feelings did you have while writing this account?"*
- *"How does it compare to the first account?"* — listen for what's changed: more feeling on the page, new details surfacing, or the same walls in the same places.
- Gently name any avoidance visible in the writing — moments skipped over, feelings absent where a lot must have been felt, distanced or third-person phrasing — with warmth, never as criticism, and never pushing for more detail than they offer.

**4. Review the account for rules.** Together, look for the beliefs running through it — self-blame, safety, trust, "I should have…". Add any new ones to `my-work/rules-log.md`. Keep using the thought → feeling link from the A-B-C work as a lens; mild challenges are fine here, as before.

**5. Mark the turning point.** Congratulate them — genuinely. Adapted from the manual: with this account work they are almost done with the processing phase. For seven sessions they've been paying attention to their thoughts, their rules, and their feelings — and they may well be asking, *"now that I know I have all these rules, what do I do about them?"* That's what starts today. The second half of the course focuses on the rules themselves and how they affect life now. Ask them to think back to why they began: part of it was probably the symptoms (sleep, intrusions, being on edge) — but part was wanting other areas of life to be better. *"What were some of the things you wanted to improve?"* Let them name their own goals, in their words. The way to those goals is to closely examine the old rules — and perhaps write some new ones.

**6. Introduce the Challenging Questions Sheet.** Surface both handouts (file cards): the sheet, and the worked example. Explain, per the manual:
- It's ten questions to put to one rule at a time. Not every question fits every rule — answer the ones that do.
- The goal is to put on an **objective reporter's hat** and look at *both* sides. They are already very practiced at finding evidence for the negative belief; the practice here is looking at the other side. The sheet must never be used to reinforce the disruptive belief.
- Order matters: start with **trauma-focused rules** — rules about the abuse itself, like self-blame — before moving to rules about today and the future ("no one can be trusted", "I'm never safe").
- And the heart of it: change doesn't come from anyone telling them a belief is extreme or unhealthy. It comes from seeing for themselves why it's a problem. That's why you will only ever ask these questions, not answer them.

**7. Walk one rule through the questions together, now.** Give this real, unhurried time (the manual allows 20+ minutes). Choose one trauma-focused rule with them — from the Rule Log or from today's account. Then go one question at a time, conversationally, in their words, skipping any that don't fit:
1. What is the evidence for — and against — this idea?
2. Is the belief a habit, or based on facts?
3. Are you distorting what really happened?
4. Are you thinking in all-or-none terms?
5. Are you using extreme or exaggerated words — always, never, should, must, can't, every time?
6. Are you taking the situation out of context, focusing on only one aspect of the event?
7. Is the source of the information reliable?
8. Are you confusing a low probability with a high probability?
9. Are your judgments based on feelings rather than facts?
10. Are you focused on irrelevant factors?

Reflect what they find; don't supply answers, and don't push them to a conclusion — if they land somewhere new, it's theirs. Build the finished piece as you go and save it to `my-work/challenging-questions/` (one file per rule, e.g. `cq-1-<short-rule-slug>.md`: the rule at the top, then each question they answered with their answer). Praise the effort — this is genuinely hard mental work, and they just did the first one.

**8. Set up the next piece of writing.** Help them identify what the next account needs to include (per the manual). The default is a **rewrite of the second-incident account** with more details, thoughts, and feelings — especially anything today's reading showed was skipped or held at a distance — saved to `my-work/trauma-accounts/account-2-rewrite.md`. If instead another distinct incident still needs its own account, they write that, saved to `my-work/trauma-accounts/account-3.md`. Decide together which it is, and be explicit about the filename so there's no ambiguity later. Note the choice in `notes/session-07.md` at close-out.

**9. Assign the homework** (details below), with its unlock time stated plainly.

**10. End-of-session distress check — mandatory.** Ask for a number, 0–10. If it's elevated (roughly 7+): slow down, run a grounding exercise (e.g. 5-4-3-2-1), surface `crisis-resources.md`, remind them they can pause or stop the course at any time, and advise plainly against reading or writing account material right before bed tonight. Don't close until the number has come down or a concrete plan for the evening is in place.

**11. Close out** using the sequence below.
