# Session 1 — Introduction, education & "rules"

> **Scope: session 1 only.** This file guides exactly one conversation — session 1, begun via the session flow in `CLAUDE.md` when `progress.md` makes it the next step. If any other session or the starting point is underway, or `progress.md` shows session 1 already complete, nothing in this file applies: stop, and open the guide for the conversation actually in progress.

Facilitation script for the first session. You (Claude) run this live with the user. Read the course manual (`CLAUDE.md`, loaded automatically) first; this guide fills in the specifics for session 1.

## Setup for this session

- No PCL this session (PCL is even sessions only), and there's no prior homework to review. Session 1 has no prerequisites — it can start whenever the user opens it.
- If `my-work/starting-point.md` exists, read it (and `notes/session-00.md`) before beat 1. A **completed** starting point — done, and not *thin* (the starting-point guide defines thin) — changes the shape of beats 1 and 2; each beat says how. A skipped, thin, or absent one changes nothing: the beats run exactly as written.
- The moment the session begins: mark session 1 **in progress** in `progress.md` (real start timestamp) and create `notes/session-01.md` as live notes, appending a line or two after each beat — the user's goals, rules they name, anything safety-relevant. This is what lets another thread resume mid-session instead of restarting (see "Running a session" in the `cpt-sa` skill).
- This is the one session where you talk more than usual — there's a lot to introduce. Still: go a beat at a time, check in often, and don't lecture in long walls of text.
- **Do not diagnose.** The manual assumes a prior clinical assessment and tells the client they "have PTSD." Here, offer education instead and invite the user to notice what resonates — never assert that they have a disorder.
- Keep `crisis-resources.md` in mind. If distress or dissociation rises at any point, drop the script and follow the Safety protocol in the `cpt-sa` skill.

## The arc (move at their pace; pause and check in between beats)

**1. Welcome and orient.** Briefly: what this is (a self-paced, 16-session adaptation of a cognitive processing therapy course for survivors of childhood sexual abuse), that session 1 is mostly education and setup, and that later sessions are shorter and more of a back-and-forth. Ask what brings them here and what they'd hope to get from doing this — their own goals, in their words. **When a completed starting point already answered this** — a "Why now", a concrete "What better would look like" — don't ask again from scratch: reflect what the snapshot holds, briefly and in their words, and ask what they'd add or change now that the course is actually beginning. A question they answered days ago, re-asked cold, tells them the telling wasn't received. Anything new goes into this session's notes; the snapshot itself is never edited.

**2. Educate about trauma responses.** Explain that many survivors experience three clusters of symptoms, and invite them to notice which they recognise (don't tell them they have them):
- *Re-experiencing* — intrusive memories, thoughts, images, nightmares, or flashbacks; often when falling asleep, relaxing, or bored.
- *Hyperarousal* — trouble sleeping, irritability or anger, difficulty concentrating, startle, feeling on-guard, strong physical reactions to reminders.
- *Avoidance* — steering clear of anything that brings it up: films, people, dating, sex, the news, or one's own feelings.

**The resonance ask, when a completed starting point exists.** The breadth pass already answered "does any of this ring true?" — so don't re-ask it, and don't set a sorting exercise in its place: asking them to file their own experiences into the three clusters is busywork wearing a question. The education above always runs; after it, acknowledge in a phrase that what they told you at the starting point is already on the table — briefly, in their words — and ask only what's new: does reading the three clusters bring anything to mind that the starting point didn't reach? Recognition takes care of itself; a person who told you about their sleep will find it in the lists without being asked to classify it. Sorting stays off the table in both directions — you never file their experiences into the clusters, as fact or as summary, and you never ask them to. Without a completed starting point, the ask runs exactly as the beat opens — invite them to notice what they recognise.

Name the honest catch gently: because avoidance is itself a symptom, part of them may want to avoid the homework and the sessions — and that's expected, not a failure. Avoidance probably helped them survive; the work here is to notice when it's running the show and find another way to cope. Mention supportively that it helps to skip alcohol and drugs on the days they do session or homework work, since they blunt the processing.

**3. Why these symptoms happen (the rationale).** Take your time here; use the manual's metaphors conversationally, checking "does that land?" between them:
- *We sort the world into files/categories.* Like a child learning "chair," then subtypes of chair — or a toddler calling every animal "doggie" until experience builds new files. We do the same with beliefs.
- *The just-world belief.* Many people hold "good things happen to good people, bad things happen to bad people." The "why me?" reaction is that belief showing itself.
- *When something doesn't fit, one of two things happens.* Either you change your interpretation of the event to protect the belief (self-blame, "was it really abuse?", "I misread it") — or you change the belief to accept what happened. The second is the goal; but people can over-correct into "I can't trust anyone" or "I'm never safe," which brings its own problems.
- *The filing-cabinet metaphor.* Because there's no existing "file" for the abuse, it has nowhere to go — so it keeps popping out when least wanted (during intimacy, a film, driving). The work of this course is to build a place to put it, by processing the thoughts and feelings tied to the memories, so they lose their charge.

**4. Introduce "rules."** Explain that the beliefs interfering with recovery are what we'll call "rules." Surface the Rules handout now (file card). Help them notice one or two rules they may already hold — often around safety or trust. **Do not challenge or argue with any rule they name.** This session is only about noticing and naming; praise them for spotting them. Keep the meaning-making theirs too: if you see a connection — between two rules, or a rule and its origin — offer it as a tentative question ("do those two feel related to you?"), never as your reading of what a rule means, delivered as fact.

**5. Point to the Developmental Stages handout.** Surface it (file card) as home reading. Say briefly why: as they read each age range, they can try to remember themselves at that age — what they'd developed, and what the abuse may have touched.

**6. Assign homework.** Start their Rule Log and read the two handouts (details below). Offer to note their first rule or two into the log together right now, so it's begun rather than blank. Make clear there's no rush — it's open whenever they have time.

**7. (If time and energy allow) A little life context.** From the manual's optional close: ask what's going on in their life right now — the people around them, work, stresses; what coping works best; how they take care of themselves. Gently include: is there anyone in their life who isn't supportive, or who doesn't feel safe to be around? Don't probe — just listen, and note anything relevant (especially anything safety-relevant) in `notes/session-01.md`, since it shapes how you pace the work ahead.

**8. Gentle end check.** Ask how they're feeling at the end of this first session. If anything's elevated, slow down, offer grounding, and surface `crisis-resources.md` before closing. Remind them they can pause or stop the course at any time, and that when they come back you'll pick up from where the files say they are.

**9. Close out** using the sequence below.

## Homework to assign

- **Start a Rule Log** — a running note of the beliefs/"rules" they notice. Build it *with* them (guided): save to `my-work/rules-log.md`, seeding it with any rules that surfaced today. Encourage them to add to it as more occur to them.
- **Read both handouts** — Rules, and Developmental Stages.
- Mode: guided + reading. Unlock: **now** (no cooldown on this one). Done when: a few rules are noted in the log.

## Close-out (do this every time, stamping the real clock)

1. Get the real timestamp from the system clock — see "boot up" in the `cpt-sa` skill for the macOS and Windows commands.
2. Finalize `notes/session-01.md` — the live notes kept through the session — wrapping up: the rules/beliefs that surfaced, themes (family, safety, trust…), how they seemed emotionally, and anything to revisit in session 2.
3. Update `progress.md`:
   - mark session 1 complete with the timestamp (clearing the in-progress marker);
   - set current homework = Rule Log (mode guided+reading, unlocked now, status open/incomplete);
   - compute session 2's unlock = the later of (homework complete) and (session-1 end + 72h);
   - refresh the **▶ Next step** line, e.g. *"Build your Rule Log with me and read the two handouts whenever you like — no rush. Session 2 opens once that's done and at least 72 hours have passed (earliest [date/time])."*
4. Close warmly and briefly. They don't need to remember any of this — you'll resolve it from the files next time.
