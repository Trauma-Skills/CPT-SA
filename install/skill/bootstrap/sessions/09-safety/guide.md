## The arc (move at their pace; pause and check in between beats)

A caution before you start: beats 4–7 below are **teaching beats**, and today carries more of them than any session since session 1. Teaching means the content is actually *said*, in your own words, across a few short conversational messages each — the adolescence reflection spoken, the five themes named and explained, the worksheet's columns walked with the worked example. A sentence announcing the topic, or a claim that it's covered, does not run the beat; if the teaching never appeared on screen, it didn't happen.

**1. Review the thinking-patterns homework.** Open with warmth and go over `my-work/thinking-patterns.md` together. For each pattern they matched to a rule, ask them to explain *how* the rule fits that pattern — there's no right or wrong answer; what matters is that they can say how a pattern fits for them. Praise the effort this took. One gentle check: the patterns are tools for *challenging* problematic beliefs, not for supporting them — if a pattern is being used as evidence ("see, I really am broken"), slow down and look at that together.

**2. Briefly review the Challenging Questions work.** Look at what's in `my-work/challenging-questions/`. Keep it brief if it went well; spend real time here if they found it hard, or if the questions are being used to perpetuate negative self-beliefs rather than loosen them.

**3. Any additional writing, and the daily reading.** If they've written anything further in `my-work/trauma-accounts/`, invite them to share it here at their own pace — no pressure. Ask: *"What thoughts and feelings did you have while writing this?"* Keep encouraging them to feel their feelings and to read straight through without stopping. Review anything shared for rules, and add new ones to the Rule Log. Ask how the daily reading of the accounts has been going, and how they've been feeling around it.

**4. The adolescence conversation.** Offer this reflection conversationally, in your own words, close to the manual's: challenging your beliefs, figuring out where they came from, and deciding whether they're helpful can be really rough — some people going through it say they don't know who they are anymore. That process is very like adolescence: working out who you are, what you like and don't like, what you think of other people, who you can and can't trust. Many abuse survivors didn't get the luxury of doing that as teenagers, because all their strength and energy went into survival — into just making it to the next day. So they're going through that discovery process now, and it can be scary and anxiety-producing. It's similar for everyone, whatever their age — and now they have all the wisdom of adulthood to guide them through it. Then genuinely check in: *"Does this make sense to you? Does it fit at all with what you're going through?"* (If it lands, it may be worth returning to in later sessions — note it.)

**5. Name the new shape of the work.** Tell them plainly: starting today, the course explores five main areas of belief, one at a time — safety, trust, power & control, esteem, and intimacy. Each session from here follows the same pattern: review the worksheets from homework, talk through the current area, then introduce the next one and practise on it. Today's area is safety.

**6. Introduce the Challenging Beliefs Worksheet.** Surface `handouts/Challenging Beliefs Worksheet.md` (file card). Explain that this is the master worksheet for the rest of the course — it pulls together everything they've built: the A-B-C chain (columns A–C), the Challenging Questions (column D), the Problematic Thinking Patterns (column E), and then goes further — building an alternative thought (F), re-rating the old one (G), and noticing what the feelings do (H). Walk through the worked flying example in the handout together, column by column, checking it makes sense before moving on. Remind them: worksheets here are conversations with you, never forms to fill alone.

**7. Introduce Safety.** Surface `handouts/Safety issues.md` (file card). Talk through the two sides, inviting them to notice which resonates rather than lecturing:
- *Beliefs about the self* — whether you can protect yourself and have some control over events. If life kept proving you couldn't, the abuse confirms it; if you once believed "it can't happen to me," the abuse shatters it. Either way it can show up as persistent anxiety, intrusive thoughts about danger, irritability, startle, and intense fear of it happening again.
- *Beliefs about others* — how dangerous people are, and whether they intend harm. Confirmed or shattered the same way; it tends to show up as avoidance and social withdrawal.
- The goal in each case is a balanced belief that mirrors reality — the "possible self-statements" in the handout — not forced positivity: some danger exists, *and* they can take steps; some people cause harm, *and* not everyone is out to harm them.

Then look at the Rule Log (`my-work/rules-log.md`) and the beliefs from recent homework together: which of their rules seem to fit the safety theme, on either side?

**8. Practise a CBW together on one safety belief.** Pick one safety-related belief with them (from the Rule Log, the recent homework, or a current event that stirred it). Walk it through the worksheet as a conversation — one column at a time, each column a question, waiting for their answer before moving on:
- **A** — *"What's the situation — the event, thought, or belief that leads to the unpleasant feeling?"*
- **B** — *"What's the thought or rule there? How much do you believe it, 0–100%?"*
- **C** — *"What do you feel when you think that — and how strongly, 0–100% for each?"*
- **D** — work through the Challenging Questions against the thought (evidence for and against, habit or fact, all-or-none, extreme, out of context, source, probability, feelings vs facts…). Let questions that don't apply pass quickly; linger where there's traction.
- **E** — *"Looking at your patterns sheet — is this one of your patterns of thinking at work? Which?"*
- **F** — *"What else could you say instead? How else could you interpret it? How much do you believe that, 0–100%?"* (Theirs, not yours — offer the question, not the answer.)
- **G** — *"Now re-rate the original thought — how much do you believe it now?"*
- **H** — *"And what do you feel now, 0–100%?"*

Save the finished sheet to `my-work/cbw/cbw-safety-1.md`. Praise the work — the first CBW is a real milestone. Don't oversell any shift in the ratings; small movement, or none, is normal on a first sheet.

**9. (If time and energy allow) Anger.** An optional topic from the manual, only if it fits where they are: anger is a common emotion at work and at home when feeling "out of control." Ask gently: *"How is that similar to your childhood — to having no control over the abuse?"* The anger example in the CBW handout can support this. Don't force it; skip if the session is already full.

**10. Assign homework** (details below).

**11. Gentle end check.** Ask how they're feeling as the session closes. If the account-sharing or safety material ran heavy, do the full 0–10 distress check; otherwise a plain check-in is enough. If it's elevated (≈7+): slow down, offer grounding, surface `crisis-resources.md`, and advise against reading the accounts right before bed. Remind them they can pause or stop the course at any time.

**12. Close out** using the sequence below.
